Does GPT-2 know how big a galaxy is?

Probing a small language model for physical-scale intuitions across length, time, and mass — and finding that one of these is encoded cleanly and the other two aren’t.

Abstract

A natural extension of the integer-magnitude axis I found in GPT-2’s embedding: does the same model represent physical magnitude — sizes, durations, masses — on a log axis? Probing 45 entities spanning many orders of magnitude in each of three categories, I find a clean asymmetry. Time is represented on a log axis with held-out R^2 = 0.81. Length is weak (R^2 = 0.18). Mass is essentially noise (R^2 = 0.03). Cross-category transfer is mostly negative. The reason is mostly linguistic, not physical: time words form a conventional one-dimensional ladder of duration (nanosecond → minute → year → millennium), so the embedding can compress them onto a single axis. Length and mass words are semantically heterogeneous — kilogram, elephant, and electron are first a unit, an animal, and a particle, and only secondarily masses — so the embedding ends up encoding category rather than magnitude.

The setup

In a previous analysis, I demonstrated that the token embedding layer of GPT-2 small represents integer magnitude along a roughly logarithmic axis (R^2 = 0.95 for \log_2(x)). This raises a follow-up question: does the same model represent physical magnitudes—such as spatial dimensions, durations, and masses—using a similar logarithmic structure?

The expected outcome is not immediately clear. On one hand, GPT-2’s training data includes texts discussing scales ranging from subatomic particles (10^{-10} m) to humans (\sim 1 m) and galaxies (10^{21} m). Consolidating these scales into a single “small-to-big” axis would allow the model to efficiently encode approximately 30 orders of magnitude along a single vector direction. On the other hand, terms like electron, kilogram, galaxy, and marathon primarily signify distinct semantic categories (particles, units, celestial structures, and athletic events). Their associated physical dimensions are secondary attributes that the model may not have isolated into a distinct geometric direction at the embedding layer.

To evaluate this, I compiled three lists of physical entities with verified SI values:

  • Length (19 entities, \log_{10} values spanning -10 to +21): atom, virus, cell, meter, inch, mile, marathon, earth, sun, lightyear, parsec, galaxy, etc.
  • Time (13 entities, \log_{10} values spanning -9 to +10.5): nanosecond, millisecond, second, minute, hour, day, month, year, decade, century, millennium, etc.
  • Mass (13 entities, \log_{10} values spanning -30 to +30): electron, proton, gram, ounce, pound, kilogram, ton, elephant, whale, earth, sun.

For each entity, I extracted the embedding vector for the token " {name}" (using a single leading space) from the layer 0 residual stream. Given the small sample sizes, I applied leave-one-out cross-validation to train a ridge regression probe.

We evaluate two competing hypotheses:

  • H1 (Dimension-specific axes): Each physical dimension occupies an independent logarithmic axis. Probes trained on one category will fail to generalize to the others.
  • H2 (Universal magnitude axis): The model relies on a shared, unit-agnostic “small-to-big” direction, meaning a single probe can predict relative scale across length, time, and mass simultaneously.

The result

The primary performance metrics are summarized below:

Category Within-category log R^2 Within-category linear R^2
Time 0.81 -0.07
Length 0.18 -0.19
Mass 0.03 -0.27

The probe successfully resolves time on a clear logarithmic scale. Conversely, the representation of length is poorly defined, and mass is statistically indistinguishable from noise.

Cross-category transfer is consistently weak or negative. The only modest exception is the time → mass transfer, which yields an R^2 = +0.19—statistically marginal, but higher than the other cross-category configurations. A unified probe trained across all 45 entities achieves an R^2 = 0.26, indicating that while some shared variance exists, it is fundamentally weak.

Consequently, neither initial hypothesis is fully supported. There is no clean, universal magnitude axis, yet individual dimension-specific log axes do not consistently emerge either. Instead, the model exhibits an asymmetrical representation: a robust log-time axis, a weak log-length representation, and no discernible log-mass encoding at the embedding level.

Why time works and the others don’t

The performance disparity suggests that the representation of time is driven by linguistic structure rather than physical principles. In English, temporal terms form an explicit, linear hierarchy:

nanosecond → microsecond → millisecond → second → minute → hour → day → week → month → year → decade → century → millennium

These tokens are highly interchangeable syntactically and semantically. For example, the phrase "for five {time-word}" remains grammatically and structurally coherent across almost every entry in the sequence. Because these words share nearly all semantic attributes—functioning primarily as temporal units that modify duration—the embedding layer can efficiently compress their variance along a single geometric direction representing scale.

Length and mass terms lack a comparable linear linguistic hierarchy. The length dataset contains a heterogeneous mixture of categories:

  • Units of measurement (millimeter, inch, meter, mile, lightyear, parsec)
  • Physical objects defined by their scale (atom, virus, cell, galaxy)
  • Specific macro-entities (earth, sun, moon, marathon)

Because these tokens span diverse semantic classes, the embedding for a token like atom primarily encodes its identity as a physical particle rather than its specific spatial dimension (10^{-10} m). Similarly, marathon is embedded primarily as a sporting event rather than a representation of a 42 km distance.

The mass category exhibits even greater semantic variance. It groups subatomic particles (electron, proton), standard weights (gram, pound, kilogram), animals (elephant, whale), and astronomical bodies (earth, sun). Because the shared attribute of mass is a tertiary feature of these tokens, the embedding layer organizes them by their primary semantic category rather than extracting their mass magnitude onto a shared axis.

NoteThe cleaner test, which I haven’t run

If this linguistic explanation holds, probing the representations with clarifying context should alter the results. Rather than feeding isolated tokens, presenting a template like "The mass of a typical {entity} is" and probing the residual stream at the final token should force the model to isolate the dimension of interest. This contextual constraint should yield cleaner magnitude representations for both length and mass.

What it tells you about LLM “physics intuition”

It is sometimes argued that large language models internalize structured, coherent models of physical reality purely from textual associations. For this claim to hold, a model would need to maintain an abstracted representation of scale—an understanding that astronomical objects are large across both spatial and mass dimensions, subatomic entities are uniformly small, and temporal descriptors map to continuous durations.

This experiment serves as a minimal baseline test for that capacity. The results indicate that GPT-2 small constructs a structured representation for time, but fails to do so for length or mass at the foundational layer. A unit-agnostic scale axis is mostly absent (combined R^2 = 0.26), and cross-category generalizations are largely unsuccessful.

This negative finding requires careful interpretation. The baseline evaluation relies strictly on uncontextualized token embeddings at layer 0 using a limited dataset, meaning it only evaluates whether these axes are explicitly organized at the initial entry point of the network. It is highly probable that:

  • Higher-capacity models (such as Pythia-1.4B or Llama variants) possess sufficient geometric dimensionality to isolate scale metrics from primary semantic categories.
  • Providing explicit contextual prompts isolates the targeted physical dimension, shifting the residual stream toward scale tracking.
  • Evaluating layers deeper within the network will show improvement, as intermediate transformer blocks integrate context and map abstract properties onto the token representations.

Thus, the baseline conclusion is specific: at the embedding layer, GPT-2 encodes a structured log-time axis but conflates length and mass magnitudes with primary token semantics. The model resolves temporal scales more readily than spatial or mass scales because the corresponding English vocabulary is structured as a clear, one-dimensional ladder.

A specific prediction — and the test

The linguistic-ladder hypothesis predicts that contextual prompts will expose the latent scale information for length and mass. By modifying the input from an isolated token to a structured prompt like "The mass of a typical {entity} is about", the transformer layers should process the underlying physical attribute before reaching the final token ("about"). This structural constraint should surface the hidden magnitude information along a linear direction accessible to linear probes.

To verify this, I re-ran the evaluations using contextualized inputs. The prompt template was set to "The {size|duration|mass} of a typical {entity} is about", and the residual stream was probed at the final token position across all network layers.

ImportantPrediction confirmed

Introducing contextual prompts causes the peak layer performance for mass to rise from 0.45 to 0.84 R^2. Time increases from 0.88 to 0.95, and length improves from 0.30 to 0.48. This confirms that the underlying scale data is accessible within the model; the uncontextualized embedding token simply stores it orthogonal to a standard linear probe direction.

The layer-by-layer performance profiles reveal different processing dynamics:

  • Uncontextualized time peaks early at layer 2 and steadily decays in subsequent layers. This indicates that temporal scale information is encoded directly within the initial token representations and becomes diffused during deeper network processing. The temporal hierarchy is natively embedded in the token relationships.
  • Contextualized attributes (time, length, and mass) uniformly peak at or near the final network layer. This confirms that the scale representations are dynamically computed and assembled across successive transformer blocks rather than being retrieved from the embedding layer.
  • Uncontextualized mass rises from an R^2 = 0.03 at layer 0 to a peak of 0.45 at layer 9 before declining. Even without explicit prompt steering, the intermediate layers partially isolate the mass attribute from raw token semantics, though less effectively than the context-steered model.

In summary, GPT-2 preserves physical scale information across all three tested dimensions, but only encodes it natively at the embedding layer for time. Resolving length and mass scales requires contextual processing across the network blocks. The structural difference is real, but it reflects how the information is routed rather than its complete absence. Linear probing on bare tokens measures embedding organization, not the limits of the model’s downstream capacity.

What’s next

Following the verification of the context-driven retrieval, several lines of inquiry open up:

Dimensional analysis: Does GPT-2 maintain a structured representation of units? If prompted with "velocity has units of", can its internal representations distinguish dimensionally valid expressions like m/s from dimensionally wrong ones like s/m? Probing whether the residual stream can linearize dimensional formulas would test its structural grasp of unit equations.

Order-of-magnitude estimation: Evaluating standard Fermi approximation problems (e.g., estimating regional labor statistics) can test whether the model executes multi-step scale arithmetic. If prior findings regarding log-axis shortcuts hold true, the network should resolve these calculations via vector addition along its internal logarithmic scales.

Cross-modal scale alignment: Evaluating multimodal models (such as CLIP variants) could determine if a shared “scale vector” aligns corresponding textual and visual tokens (e.g., mapping an image of a cellular structure to an image of a planetary system). A model exposed to diverse multimodal inputs may learn a clean scale axis from visual framing cues like magnification or perspective.

Direct isolation of the linguistic-ladder effect: This hypothesis can be isolated by probing abstract concept hierarchies that form explicit verbal scales independently of physical units. For instance, testing temperature sequences (cold, cool, warm, hot, boiling) or velocity descriptors (slow, walking, running, flying) should yield clean linear probe performance at layer 0, whereas semantic categories that lack a linear hierarchy should uniformly fail.
