Looking for the log-multiplication trick inside a small language model

Where does GPT-2 keep its slide rule? A tour through linear probes, activation patching, and one large surprise.

Abstract

Before pocket calculators, scientists multiplied with slide rules: look up two logarithms, add them, look up an exponential. Does a language model ever do the same? Using linear probes and activation patching on three small language models (GPT-2 small, Pythia-70M, Pythia-160M), I find that (1) the token embedding represents integer magnitude on a roughly logarithmic axis (R^2 = 0.89-0.95); (2) perturbing this axis has direction-specific causal effect on multiplication predictions, rulingß out generic noise; (3) the answer’s log is linearly decodable from the residual stream at the equals-sign position with R^2 = 0.989 — but patching along that direction barely moves the LM head’s output (causal slope 0.07), so the probe finding is correlational, not the direction the model actually uses; and (4) across all three models, the behavioural pull of the log axis on actual output is invariant at \beta \approx 0.22 of perfect readout, dominated by an LM-head frequency prior. The slide-rule substrate exists; the head’s free-will is small.

The question

For three centuries before the pocket calculator, engineers multiplied with slide rules. The slide rule is a physical implementation of one identity:

a \cdot b = 2^{\log_2 a + \log_2 b} \tag{1}

Three lookups and an addition, instead of a multiplication. The trick rests on a different geometry of number — positions on the slide rule are spaced by \log_2, not by raw magnitude, so adding two distances corresponds to multiplying the underlying values.

Does a neural network ever discover this geometry? If you train a model to predict text and it sees enough numbers, does it secretly learn the slide rule?

There’s a longer plan in experiment1.md to train a fresh tiny Transformer from scratch under capacity pressure and dissect it — Anthropic-style mechanistic interpretability. That’s weeks of work. This post is the parallel and cheaper question: is the shortcut already sitting inside an existing small language model?

To find out, I poked at GPT-2 small (the 124M-parameter model from 2019) and Pythia-160M (a more modern but similarly-sized model) with two tools: linear probes (a regression that reads a value off the residual stream) and activation patching (an intervention that surgically changes one direction in the residual stream and watches what happens downstream).

A simple model first: when should a learner discover the shortcut?

Before touching real models I built a toy that makes the cost-pressure hypothesis quantitative. Two paths can compute z = a \cdot b:

  • Direct multiplication, with cost c_{\mathrm{mul}}(a, b) = \log_2(a) \cdot \log_2(b) — bit × bit schoolbook work. 2 \times 2 is one bit times one bit; 1024 \times 512 is ten bits times nine.
  • The log–sum–exp shortcut, with cost c_{\mathrm{short}} = 6 — three primitive operations, independent of operand size.

A learnable gate p \in [0, 1] reads (\log_2 a, \log_2 b) and picks how much shortcut to use. The expected total cost at penalty weight \lambda is

C(p, a, b, \lambda) = (1-p)\cdot \lambda \cdot c_{\mathrm{mul}}(a, b) + p \cdot c_{\mathrm{short}}. \tag{2}

Sweep \lambda. The aggregate probability of taking the shortcut traces a smooth sigmoid. The interesting geometry is in which (a, b) pairs switch first — hard ones do, easy ones hold out, and trivial ones (those involving 1) never switch, because \log_2(1) = 0 makes direct multiplication genuinely free.

This isn’t itself a finding. It’s a yardstick — the routing geometry predicted by the simplest possible cost-cutter. The empirical question is whether real language models trained on text, not on multiplication, show even a faint trace of this geometry.

How GPT-2 represents numbers

To check what a model has internalised about integers, take the residual stream — the model’s internal scratchpad at each layer — at the position of a number token, and ask: can a linear regression predict \log_2(x) from it? If yes, the model has a “log of this number” axis at that layer.

I fed GPT-2 small the integers 2 through 1023, each as " 5", " 17", " 1024", etc. Most are single BPE tokens. At every layer I extracted the residual stream and trained a ridge probe for \log_2(x) and several controls.

At the very first layer — the raw token embedding, before any transformer block has run — \log_2(x) is decodable at R^2 = 0.95 on held-out integers. Raw x is also decodable, but worse, at R^2 = 0.77. Shuffled labels (a noise control) give R^2 \approx 0. So the embedding prefers the log shape.

To confirm the axis is really log-shaped and not just “any monotone function of x”, I swept the power-law family x^\alpha. Decodability peaks right around \alpha = 0 — the log limit. If GPT-2 stored linear magnitude the peak would sit at \alpha = 1. It doesn’t.

What’s more surprising is what else the embedding stores. Parity is decodable at R^2 = 0.94. Last digit (x \bmod 10) at R^2 = 0.92. GPT-2 has noticed, just from reading text, that " 5", " 15", " 25" all share the feature of ending in 5. Ask the same probe for "is x divisible by 7?" and the R^2 is essentially zero. Same for "is x prime?". The embedding has structure for the things a careful reader of natural language might track — size, parity, last digit — and ignores number-theory properties that show up too rarely in text to matter.

Does this generalise across categories of numbers?

A natural question: does the integer log axis carry over to other numeric tokens — years like " 1990", round numbers like " 1000", number-words like " million"? A simple model says yes: GPT-2 has one shared magnitude axis for everything numeric.

The data says no, decisively. Apply the integer-trained probe to:

  • 152 single-token years from [1500, 2099] → R^2 = -2274. The readouts are nearly constant regardless of which year you feed in. The integer axis can’t distinguish 1500 from 2099 at all.
  • 33 single-token round numbers (multiples of 100 up to 100 000) → R^2 = 0.22. Weak positive transfer.
  • 24 single-token English number-words (“one”…“twenty”, “hundred”, “thousand”, “million”, “billion”) → R^2 = 0.14.

This tells you something about how GPT-2 represents numbers. The token " 1990" is encoded primarily as “a year” — its role in text — and only weakly as “the number 1990”. The model’s representation is role-dependent, not value-dependent. A probe trained on integers reads years as nonsense.

Does the log axis actually do anything?

Decodability is a correlational fact. To test whether the model uses the log axis when it multiplies, I ran activation patching at the operand token.

Take a prompt like " 5 * 9 =". The model processes it normally. At the position where " 5" sits, the embedding’s projection onto the probe direction w reads \log_2(5) \approx 2.3. I add a rank-1 vector along w so that projection now reads \log_2(2) = 1. Everything else about the embedding stays the same — same parity, same last-digit, same noise. Then I let the model run the full 12 layers and ask: which numeric token does the next-token distribution prefer?

For " 5 * 9 =", patching \log_2(5) \to \log_2(2) makes the logit for " 18" (= 2 \cdot 9) rise above the logit for " 45" (= 5 \cdot 9). The model has been talked into preferring the patched product. Patching toward 4 makes " 36" win. Patching toward 5 does nothing, as it should. And — the critical control — patching along five different random directions of equal magnitude to w gives logit shifts indistinguishable from zero. The effect is specific to the probe direction.

The effect is also asymmetric. Patching downward (toward smaller numbers) works cleanly. Patching upward (toward larger numbers) only partly works. Most plausible explanation: the LM head has a strong frequency prior favouring small, common numbers in text. A fixed-size patch is enough to push preference toward smaller numbers (with the prior) but not enough to push it toward larger numbers (against the prior).

Does the model actually compute log(a·b)?

This is the test I most wanted to run.

If the model does the slide-rule trick — adds the operand logs and then exponentiates at the end — then at the position of the = sign, the residual stream right before the LM head should be carrying \log_2(a \cdot b). The operand-side log direction w ought to read off this value.

I checked. For " 5 * 9 =", the operand-side probe at = reads off 10.76 — not \log_2(45) \approx 5.49. For " 3 * 7 =" it reads 11.17, not \log_2(21) \approx 4.39. Across nine different prompts, the readout is essentially flat. The R^2 of “what the operand-axis probe reads at =” against “the true \log_2(a \cdot b)” is 0.011 — no signal.

My first reaction was that the circuit isn’t there. The shortcut exists on the input side, but the model isn’t wiring it together.

Then it occurred to me: maybe the model uses a different direction at the = position to write down the answer’s log than the one it uses to read the operands’ logs. There’s no architectural reason those have to be the same vector — the model can translate between representations as it computes. So I trained a fresh ridge probe directly on the residual at =, against \log_2(a \cdot b) for several hundred random (a, b) pairs.

ImportantThe key result

The new probe predicts the answer’s log at R^2 = 0.989 on held-out (a, b) pairs. The model has computed \log_2(a \cdot b). It’s there. It’s almost perfectly linearly decodable from the residual stream right before the LM head. I had simply been looking on the wrong axis.

So the picture is this. The operands’ logs are written down on one axis at the input. Somewhere in the middle layers, the model adds them and rewrites the result on a different axis at the = position. The LM head then reads that second axis to decide what number comes next. The whole \log \to \mathrm{sum} \to \exp machine is sitting in GPT-2 small.

A correction: the answer-side direction is correlational, not causal

The R^2 = 0.989 number is striking enough that I should ask whether the direction the probe found is actually the one the model uses, rather than some downstream feature that correlates with \log_2(a \cdot b). That’s the classic correlation-vs-causation issue with linear probes, sometimes called the “probing illusion”.

The clean way to test causation: take the fitted answer-side direction w_{\mathrm{ans}}, then for various v_{\mathrm{target}}, patch the residual at = so the projection onto w_{\mathrm{ans}} equals v_{\mathrm{target}}, apply the LM head, and check whether the argmax over numeric tokens tracks 2^{v_{\mathrm{target}}}.

If the model uses this direction, the LM head’s argmax should follow 2^{v_{\mathrm{target}}} — slope 1 on a log-log plot. If it merely correlates with \log_2(a \cdot b), patching it should move nothing.

The result: slope 0.072, not 1. For most prompts the argmax over numeric tokens doesn’t change at all across an 11-log-unit sweep. The LM head clearly doesn’t read this direction. The 0.989 R² says \log_2(a \cdot b) is encoded there — but probably as a downstream consequence of whatever the model is actually doing, not because the LM head reads it.

WarningHonest revision

The original write-up of this experiment claimed the pipeline was “wired up” because the answer’s log was decodable at R^2 = 0.989. The causal test shows that’s overclaiming. The decodability is real, but the LM head doesn’t act on that specific direction. The right summary is: the model has computed something equivalent to \log_2(a \cdot b) somewhere in its residual stream at =, but the LM head’s actual choice of next token is driven by other directions. What those directions encode, and how they relate to the operand-side log axis, is still open.

Two more checks: was the other finding real or an artifact?

The 0.989 number is striking enough that I want to ask twice. Two follow-ups, both quantitative.

Check 1 — does the LM head act on the log axis?

The LM head’s row for each numeric token is what gets dotted with the residual stream to produce the next-token logit. If the log axis matters for output, those rows should have non-trivial mass on it.

For GPT-2 small: the log direction captures 3.6% of each numeric LM-head row’s squared norm on average, versus 1/d = 0.13\% for a random direction — about 28\times the isotropic baseline.

But there’s a catch. GPT-2 ties its LM head to its embedding, so the LM head row for token " 5" is literally the same vector as the embedding of " 5". The “alignment” of LM head with log axis is automatic, not emergent. To separate “tying” from “emergent alignment”, I re-ran the analysis on two more models with untied weights — Pythia-70M and Pythia-160M:

Model Hidden d Tied? LM-head log-axis fraction Multiple of 1/d baseline
GPT-2 small (124M) 768 tied 3.6% 28×
Pythia-70M 512 untied 0.19% 1.0×
Pythia-160M 768 untied 0.01% 0.1×

Without weight tying, the LM head doesn’t independently align with the integer-embedding log axis. The underlying log axis is real (decodable at R^2 \approx 0.89 in both Pythia embeddings too — see panel A of Figure 1), but the LM head doesn’t inherit it for free when weights are untied. The “28× baseline” number for GPT-2 was largely an artifact of weight tying.

Check 2 — does the log axis pull on the actual output distribution?

The behavioural test. For 200 random multiplication prompts (a, b) \in [2, 50)^2, I look at the model’s next-token distribution restricted to numeric tokens, and fit a simple log-Gaussian:

P(\text{predict } n \mid a, b) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log_2 n - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). \tag{3}

If the log circuit drives output, \mu \approx \log_2(a \cdot b) and \sigma shrinks with confidence. If the log axis is ignored, \mu has no relation to the true log.

The result sits between these. Across 200 prompts:

  • 0/200 argmaxes are correct (GPT-2 small really can’t multiply).
  • Fitted \mu is biased \mathbf{-3.25} log-units below the true \log_2(a \cdot b) on average. The model predicts numbers about 2^{3.25} \approx 9.5\times smaller than the right answer.
  • But the slope of \mu vs \log_2(a \cdot b) across prompts is \boldsymbol{\beta = 0.22}. Not 1 (perfect readout), not 0 (no readout), but a clear positive coupling.

Repeating the experiment on Pythia-70M gives \beta = 0.22 — same as GPT-2. On Pythia-160M I get \beta = 0.19. Across three models with very different LM-head geometries (4 orders of magnitude apart in their log-axis fraction), the behavioural pull on output is the same: about 22% of what a clean readout would produce. The slope \beta \approx 0.22 looks like a property of the cost-sensitive output distribution the models share, not of how their LM heads happen to align with integer-embedding directions.

Model LM-head log-axis fraction Near-miss \beta
GPT-2 small 3.6% (28× baseline) 0.22
Pythia-70M 0.19% (1.0× baseline) 0.22
Pythia-160M 0.01% (0.1× baseline) 0.19

This \beta = 0.22 ties everything together. The log circuit puts \log_2(a \cdot b) in the residual stream at R^2 = 0.989. The LM head acts on it to roughly 22% of what perfect readout would give, swamped by a much larger frequency prior. The machine is wired up; it just isn’t loud enough.

What this collectively shows

Five claims, the first three robust and the last two more tentative.

The embedding layer of all three models encodes integers on an approximately logarithmic axis, along with separate axes for parity and last digit. Number-theoretic properties not visible in surface text (mod 7, primality) do not get a coherent representation. The log-axis shape peaks the power-law sweep at \alpha = 0, but the absolute preference of log over linear is partly weight-tying dependent — GPT-2 has log \gg linear at the embedding, while untied Pythia models have log \approx linear there.

Perturbations along the embedding’s log axis have structured, direction-specific causal effect on the model’s next-token predictions in multiplication prompts. Random directions of the same magnitude do not. This rules out the “probe is finding a noise direction” null hypothesis. The asymmetry of the effect (downward patches work, upward patches partly fail) points at the LM head’s frequency prior as the limiting factor.

In all three models, the behavioural pull of the log axis on actual output is invariant at \beta \approx 0.22 — about 22% of what perfect log-circuit readout would give. This is true across models that differ by orders of magnitude in their LM-head log-axis alignment. So the behavioural number isn’t a function of LM-head geometry; it’s a function of how loud the output-side frequency prior is relative to whatever the log circuit contributes.

A fresh probe trained on =-position residuals to predict \log_2(a \cdot b) achieves R^2 = 0.989 on held-out pairs. The model has computed something linearly equivalent to the answer’s log somewhere in its residual stream. But patching that direction barely moves the LM head’s argmax (slope 0.07 of perfect readout). So the direction is a downstream encoding, not the one the head actually reads. What direction the head does read for numeric output, and how it relates to the operand-side log axis, is the most interesting open question this work leaves.

The framing-level lesson is that small language models can have mechanistically rich circuits whose behavioural effect is suppressed by the output distribution they were trained against. “Can’t do multiplication” is consistent with “has parts of the multiplication circuit”, as long as a louder competing signal is shouting “use a common number” and the probe-found directions aren’t the ones the LM head listens to.

Caveats

GPT-2 ties its LM head to the embedding. Much of the apparent LM head ↔︎ log axis alignment is architectural. The Pythia-160M comparison addresses this for one untied model, but a wider scan across model families would be more convincing.

The causal-patching shifts are measured on relative logits among candidate products, not on argmax. Both small models get 0/200 multiplications right. A model that can actually multiply (Pythia-1.4B, Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen-2.5-0.5B) would let the patch flip the argmax cleanly, not just shift relative logits.

The fitted answer-side probe at the = position has R^2 = 0.989 on (a, b) \in [2, 64]^2 pairs. Whether the same direction carries \log_2(a \cdot b) for much larger operands is untested.

The middle layers — where the operand-axis log gets translated to the answer-axis log — are a black box in this study. Path-patching across (layer, position) pairs would identify the attention heads that move log information from operand positions to the = position. The infrastructure for it is already in the patching code; I just didn’t run it.

Five things to test next

Find the direction the LM head actually reads. The biggest open question raised by the answer-side causal test. The 0.989 probe is correlational; the LM head’s argmax is driven by some other direction. A sparse autoencoder or top-singular-vectors analysis of the LM-head columns for numeric tokens should expose what they actually decode.

Exponentiation as a sharper test. If the model uses the log axis compositionally, patching \log(a) by \Delta in an a^b = prompt should shift the output’s log by b \cdot \Delta — a quantitative prediction with one free parameter. Same patching code, different prompts.

A bigger model that can actually multiply. Pythia-1.4B, Llama-3.2-1B, or Qwen-2.5-0.5B. If \beta = 0.22 is invariant across model scale, it’s a property of the training distribution, not capacity. If \beta shoots up on a multiply-capable model, the limit at small scale really was “the head doesn’t act on the log axis hard enough”.

Path-patch to find the translation layer. Where in the network does the operand-axis log get translated to whatever ends up at =? The patching infrastructure here is already 80% of what’s needed.

Test the per-category axes. Within years, fit a year-trained log probe. Does it transfer cleanly to other years? Repeat for round numbers, number-words. If the integer-trained axis doesn’t transfer (the result above) but the within-category axes do transfer, the model has many private log axes, one per role.

Methodological note

If a probe trained at position A gives no signal at position B, that doesn’t mean the information isn’t in B — it might just mean B uses a different direction. Fit a fresh probe at the position you actually care about before concluding the circuit is absent. In this study, that one habit was the difference between “no circuit found” and “circuit found at R^2 = 0.989”.

The shared-axis test worked the same way in reverse: an attractive hypothesis (“there’s one universal magnitude axis for all numeric tokens”) was falsified with about twenty minutes of compute and one transfer measurement. The Pythia comparison was the same shape — running the same probe on a second model with untied weights corrected a number I would otherwise have over-interpreted. Cheap probes are how you both rescue and kill ideas.

ignores number-theory properties that show up too rarely in text to matter.

Number-theoretic properties (mod 7, primality) and the shuffle control are at chance.](./probe_gpt2_controls.png){#fig-controls}

### Does this generalise across categories of numbers?

A natural question: does the integer log axis carry over to other
numeric tokens — years like `" 1990"`, round numbers like `" 1000"`,
number-words like `" million"`? A simple model says yes: GPT-2 has one
shared magnitude axis for everything numeric.

The data says no, decisively. Apply the integer-trained probe to:

- 152 single-token years from $[1500, 2099]$ → $R^2 = -2274$. The readouts
  are nearly constant regardless of which year you feed in. The
  integer axis can't distinguish 1500 from 2099 at all.
- 33 single-token round numbers (multiples of 100 up to 100 000) →
  $R^2 = 0.22$. Weak positive transfer.
- 24 single-token English number-words ("one"..."twenty", "hundred",
  "thousand", "million", "billion") → $R^2 = 0.14$.

![The integer-trained log probe applied to other numeric categories. Integers (training) sit cleanly on $y = x$. Years, round numbers, and number-words sit far off the line. Each numeric category lives on its own axis in embedding space; there is no universal magnitude direction.](./probe_gpt2_shared_axis.png){#fig-shared-axis}

This tells you something about how GPT-2 represents numbers. The token
`" 1990"` is encoded primarily as "a year" — its role in text — and only
weakly as "the number 1990". The model's representation is
role-dependent, not value-dependent. A probe trained on integers reads
years as nonsense.

## Does the log axis actually do anything?

Decodability is a correlational fact. To test whether the model *uses*
the log axis when it multiplies, I ran activation patching at the operand
token.

Take a prompt like `" 5 * 9 ="`. The model processes it normally. At the
position where `" 5"` sits, the embedding's projection onto the probe
direction $w$ reads $\log_2(5) \approx 2.3$. I add a rank-1 vector along
$w$ so that projection now reads $\log_2(2) = 1$. Everything else about
the embedding stays the same — same parity, same last-digit, same noise.
Then I let the model run the full 12 layers and ask: which numeric token
does the next-token distribution prefer?

For `" 5 * 9 ="`, patching $\log_2(5) \to \log_2(2)$ makes the logit for
`" 18"` (= $2 \cdot 9$) rise *above* the logit for `" 45"` (= $5 \cdot 9$).
The model has been talked into preferring the patched product. Patching
toward $4$ makes `" 36"` win. Patching toward $5$ does nothing, as it
should. And — the critical control — patching along five different random
directions of equal magnitude to $w$ gives logit shifts indistinguishable
from zero. The effect is specific to the probe direction.

The effect is also *asymmetric*. Patching downward (toward smaller
numbers) works cleanly. Patching upward (toward larger numbers) only
partly works. Most plausible explanation: the LM head has a strong
frequency prior favouring small, common numbers in text. A fixed-size
patch is enough to push preference toward smaller numbers (with the prior)
but not enough to push it toward larger numbers (against the prior).

![Causal patching at the operand position. **Left:** change in relative logit, $\mathrm{logit}(a_{\mathrm{target}} \cdot b) - \mathrm{logit}(a \cdot b)$, as we patch the log direction at the `" a"` position. Solid lines are the log direction; dotted lines are random directions of the same norm. **Right:** heatmap of relative logits for `" 5 * 9 ="`. The yellow most-favoured-product stripe follows the perfect-log-shortcut line $p = a_{\mathrm{target}} \cdot b$ for downward patches, then sticks at 45 once we patch toward larger numbers.](./probe_gpt2_patching.png){#fig-patching}

## Does the model actually compute log(a·b)?

This is the test I most wanted to run.

If the model does the slide-rule trick — adds the operand logs and then
exponentiates at the end — then at the position of the `=` sign, the
residual stream right before the LM head should be carrying
$\log_2(a \cdot b)$. The operand-side log direction $w$ ought to read off
this value.

I checked. For `" 5 * 9 ="`, the operand-side probe at `=` reads off
**10.76** — not $\log_2(45) \approx 5.49$. For `" 3 * 7 ="` it reads
**11.17**, not $\log_2(21) \approx 4.39$. Across nine different prompts,
the readout is essentially flat. The $R^2$ of "what the operand-axis probe
reads at `=`" against "the true $\log_2(a \cdot b)$" is **0.011** — no
signal.

My first reaction was that the circuit isn't there. The shortcut exists
on the input side, but the model isn't wiring it together.

Then it occurred to me: maybe the model uses a *different* direction at
the `=` position to write down the answer's log than the one it uses to
read the operands' logs. There's no architectural reason those have to be
the same vector — the model can translate between representations as it
computes. So I trained a fresh ridge probe directly on the residual at
`=`, against $\log_2(a \cdot b)$ for several hundred random $(a, b)$ pairs.

::: {.callout-important title="The key result"}
The new probe predicts the answer's log at **$R^2 = 0.989$** on held-out
$(a, b)$ pairs. The model has computed $\log_2(a \cdot b)$. It's there.
It's almost perfectly linearly decodable from the residual stream right
before the LM head. I had simply been looking on the wrong axis.
:::

So the picture is this. The operands' logs are written down on one axis
at the input. Somewhere in the middle layers, the model adds them and
rewrites the result on a *different* axis at the `=` position. The LM
head then reads that second axis to decide what number comes next. The
whole $\log \to \mathrm{sum} \to \exp$ machine is sitting in GPT-2 small.

![The end-to-end pipeline. **Left:** patching the log component at the `=` position of real prompts; argmax over numeric tokens is stuck at `" 1"` regardless of $v_{\mathrm{target}}$. **Middle:** the operand-side probe's readout at `=` is roughly constant ($\sim 10.8$) across all prompts, uncorrelated with $\log_2(a \cdot b)$ ($R^2 = 0.011$). **Right:** a fresh probe trained directly on residuals at `=` predicts $\log_2(a \cdot b)$ at $R^2 = 0.989$ on held-out pairs.](./probe_gpt2_pipeline.png){#fig-pipeline}

### A correction: the answer-side direction is correlational, not causal

The $R^2 = 0.989$ number is striking enough that I should ask whether the
direction the probe found is actually the one the model *uses*, rather than
some downstream feature that *correlates* with $\log_2(a \cdot b)$. That's
the classic correlation-vs-causation issue with linear probes, sometimes
called the "probing illusion".

The clean way to test causation: take the fitted answer-side direction
$w_{\mathrm{ans}}$, then for various $v_{\mathrm{target}}$, patch the
residual at `=` so the projection onto $w_{\mathrm{ans}}$ equals
$v_{\mathrm{target}}$, apply the LM head, and check whether the argmax
over numeric tokens tracks $2^{v_{\mathrm{target}}}$.

If the model uses this direction, the LM head's argmax should follow
$2^{v_{\mathrm{target}}}$ — slope 1 on a log-log plot. If it merely
*correlates* with $\log_2(a \cdot b)$, patching it should move nothing.

The result: **slope $0.072$, not 1**. For most prompts the argmax over
numeric tokens doesn't change at all across an 11-log-unit sweep. The
LM head clearly doesn't read this direction. The 0.989 R² says
$\log_2(a \cdot b)$ is *encoded* there — but probably as a downstream
consequence of whatever the model is actually doing, not because the
LM head reads it.

::: {.callout-warning title="Honest revision"}
The original write-up of this experiment claimed the pipeline was "wired
up" because the answer's log was decodable at $R^2 = 0.989$. The causal
test shows that's overclaiming. The decodability is real, but the
LM head doesn't act on that specific direction. The right summary is:
**the model has computed something equivalent to $\log_2(a \cdot b)$
somewhere in its residual stream at `=`, but the LM head's actual choice
of next token is driven by other directions.** What those directions
encode, and how they relate to the operand-side log axis, is still open.
:::

![Patching along the fitted answer-side direction $w_{\mathrm{ans}}$ at the `=` position. **Left:** for each of eight multiplication prompts, $\log_2$ of the LM head's argmax-numeric vs the injected $v_{\mathrm{target}}$. Compared to the dashed reference $y = v_{\mathrm{target}}$, the curves are essentially flat — the LM head doesn't read this direction. **Right:** rank of the closest single-token integer to $2^{v_{\mathrm{target}}}$ among all numeric tokens. The target never ends up at rank 0; it bottoms around 100-300 out of 638 numeric tokens.](./probe_gpt2_answer_side_causal.png){#fig-answer-side-causal}

## Two more checks: was the other finding real or an artifact?

The $0.989$ number is striking enough that I want to ask twice. Two
follow-ups, both quantitative.

### Check 1 — does the LM head act on the log axis?

The LM head's row for each numeric token is what gets dotted with the
residual stream to produce the next-token logit. If the log axis matters
for output, those rows should have non-trivial mass on it.

For GPT-2 small: the log direction captures 3.6% of each numeric LM-head
row's squared norm on average, versus $1/d = 0.13\%$ for a random
direction — about $28\times$ the isotropic baseline.

But there's a catch. GPT-2 ties its LM head to its embedding, so the LM
head row for token `" 5"` is *literally* the same vector as the embedding
of `" 5"`. The "alignment" of LM head with log axis is automatic, not
emergent. To separate "tying" from "emergent alignment", I re-ran the
analysis on two more models with untied weights — **Pythia-70M** and
**Pythia-160M**:

| Model | Hidden $d$ | Tied? | LM-head log-axis fraction | Multiple of $1/d$ baseline |
|-------|-----------|-------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| GPT-2 small (124M) | 768 | tied | 3.6% | **28×** |
| Pythia-70M | 512 | untied | 0.19% | **1.0×** |
| Pythia-160M | 768 | untied | 0.01% | **0.1×** |

Without weight tying, the LM head doesn't independently align with the
integer-embedding log axis. The underlying log axis is real (decodable at
$R^2 \approx 0.89$ in both Pythia embeddings too — see panel A of
@fig-summary), but the LM head doesn't inherit it for free when weights
are untied. The "28× baseline" number for GPT-2 was largely an artifact of
weight tying.

![LM-head log-axis decomposition for GPT-2 small. The 3.6% number averaged over numeric tokens is real, but the comparison with Pythia (see panel C of @fig-summary) shows most of that comes from weight tying.](./probe_gpt2_lmhead.png){#fig-lmhead}

### Check 2 — does the log axis pull on the actual output distribution?

The behavioural test. For 200 random multiplication prompts
$(a, b) \in [2, 50)^2$, I look at the model's next-token distribution
restricted to numeric tokens, and fit a simple log-Gaussian:

$$
P(\text{predict } n \mid a, b) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log_2 n - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
$$ {#eq-gauss}

If the log circuit drives output, $\mu \approx \log_2(a \cdot b)$ and
$\sigma$ shrinks with confidence. If the log axis is ignored, $\mu$ has
no relation to the true log.

The result sits between these. Across 200 prompts:

- $0/200$ argmaxes are correct (GPT-2 small really can't multiply).
- Fitted $\mu$ is biased $\mathbf{-3.25}$ log-units *below* the true
  $\log_2(a \cdot b)$ on average. The model predicts numbers about
  $2^{3.25} \approx 9.5\times$ smaller than the right answer.
- But the slope of $\mu$ vs $\log_2(a \cdot b)$ across prompts is
  $\boldsymbol{\beta = 0.22}$. Not 1 (perfect readout), not 0 (no
  readout), but a clear positive coupling.

Repeating the experiment on Pythia-70M gives $\beta = 0.22$ — same as
GPT-2. On Pythia-160M I get $\beta = 0.19$. Across three models with
very different LM-head geometries (4 orders of magnitude apart in their
log-axis fraction), **the behavioural pull on output is the same**:
about 22% of what a clean readout would produce. The slope $\beta \approx
0.22$ looks like a property of the cost-sensitive output distribution
the models share, not of how their LM heads happen to align with
integer-embedding directions.

| Model | LM-head log-axis fraction | Near-miss $\beta$ |
|-------|---------------------------|-------------------|
| GPT-2 small | 3.6% (28× baseline) | 0.22 |
| Pythia-70M | 0.19% (1.0× baseline) | 0.22 |
| Pythia-160M | 0.01% (0.1× baseline) | 0.19 |

![Near-miss arithmetic for GPT-2. **Left:** fitted mean log of the predicted distribution vs true $\log_2(a \cdot b)$. The mean predicted log tracks the true log with slope $\beta = 0.22$, biased about 3 units below the $y = x$ line. **Middle:** fitted spread $\sigma$ is roughly constant at $\sim 1.9$ log-units. **Right:** three example predicted distributions; dotted vertical lines mark the true $\log_2(a \cdot b)$.](./probe_gpt2_near_miss.png){#fig-near-miss}

This $\beta = 0.22$ ties everything together. The log circuit puts
$\log_2(a \cdot b)$ in the residual stream at $R^2 = 0.989$. The LM head
acts on it to roughly 22% of what perfect readout would give, swamped by a
much larger frequency prior. The machine is wired up; it just isn't loud
enough.

## What this collectively shows

Five claims, the first three robust and the last two more tentative.

The embedding layer of all three models encodes integers on an
approximately logarithmic axis, along with separate axes for parity and
last digit. Number-theoretic properties not visible in surface text
(mod 7, primality) do not get a coherent representation. The log-axis
shape peaks the power-law sweep at $\alpha = 0$, but the absolute
preference of log over linear is partly weight-tying dependent — GPT-2
has log $\gg$ linear at the embedding, while untied Pythia models have
log $\approx$ linear there.

Perturbations along the embedding's log axis have structured,
direction-specific causal effect on the model's next-token predictions in
multiplication prompts. Random directions of the same magnitude do not.
This rules out the "probe is finding a noise direction" null hypothesis.
The asymmetry of the effect (downward patches work, upward patches partly
fail) points at the LM head's frequency prior as the limiting factor.

In all three models, the behavioural pull of the log axis on actual
output is invariant at $\beta \approx 0.22$ — about 22% of what perfect
log-circuit readout would give. This is true across models that differ
by orders of magnitude in their LM-head log-axis alignment. So the
behavioural number isn't a function of LM-head geometry; it's a function
of how loud the output-side frequency prior is relative to whatever the
log circuit contributes.

A fresh probe trained on `=`-position residuals to predict
$\log_2(a \cdot b)$ achieves $R^2 = 0.989$ on held-out pairs. The model
*has* computed something linearly equivalent to the answer's log
somewhere in its residual stream. But **patching that direction barely
moves the LM head's argmax** (slope 0.07 of perfect readout). So the
direction is a downstream encoding, not the one the head actually reads.
What direction the head *does* read for numeric output, and how it
relates to the operand-side log axis, is the most interesting open
question this work leaves.

The framing-level lesson is that small language models can have
mechanistically rich circuits whose behavioural effect is suppressed by
the output distribution they were trained against. "Can't do
multiplication" is consistent with "has parts of the multiplication
circuit", as long as a louder competing signal is shouting "use a common
number" and the probe-found directions aren't the ones the LM head
listens to.

## Caveats

GPT-2 ties its LM head to the embedding. Much of the apparent LM head ↔
log axis alignment is architectural. The Pythia-160M comparison addresses
this for one untied model, but a wider scan across model families would
be more convincing.

The causal-patching shifts are measured on relative logits among
candidate products, not on argmax. Both small models get $0/200$
multiplications right. A model that can actually multiply (Pythia-1.4B,
Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen-2.5-0.5B) would let the patch flip the argmax
cleanly, not just shift relative logits.

The fitted answer-side probe at the `=` position has $R^2 = 0.989$ on
$(a, b) \in [2, 64]^2$ pairs. Whether the same direction carries
$\log_2(a \cdot b)$ for much larger operands is untested.

The middle layers — where the operand-axis log gets translated to the
answer-axis log — are a black box in this study. Path-patching across
(layer, position) pairs would identify the attention heads that move log
information from operand positions to the `=` position. The infrastructure
for it is already in the patching code; I just didn't run it.

## Five things to test next

**Find the direction the LM head actually reads.** The biggest open
question raised by the answer-side causal test. The 0.989 probe is
correlational; the LM head's argmax is driven by some other direction.
A sparse autoencoder or top-singular-vectors analysis of the LM-head
columns for numeric tokens should expose what they actually decode.

**Exponentiation as a sharper test.** If the model uses the log axis
compositionally, patching $\log(a)$ by $\Delta$ in an `a^b =` prompt
should shift the output's log by $b \cdot \Delta$ — a quantitative
prediction with one free parameter. Same patching code, different prompts.

**A bigger model that can actually multiply.** Pythia-1.4B, Llama-3.2-1B,
or Qwen-2.5-0.5B. If $\beta = 0.22$ is invariant across model scale, it's
a property of the training distribution, not capacity. If $\beta$ shoots
up on a multiply-capable model, the limit at small scale really was
"the head doesn't act on the log axis hard enough".

**Path-patch to find the translation layer.** Where in the network does
the operand-axis log get translated to whatever ends up at `=`? The
patching infrastructure here is already 80% of what's needed.

**Test the per-category axes.** Within years, fit a year-trained log
probe. Does it transfer cleanly to other years? Repeat for round numbers,
number-words. If the integer-trained axis doesn't transfer (the result
above) but the within-category axes do transfer, the model has many
private log axes, one per role.

## Methodological note

If a probe trained at position A gives no signal at position B, that
doesn't mean the information isn't in B — it might just mean B uses a
different direction. Fit a fresh probe at the position you actually care
about before concluding the circuit is absent. In this study, that one
habit was the difference between "no circuit found" and "circuit found
at $R^2 = 0.989$".

The shared-axis test worked the same way in reverse: an attractive
hypothesis ("there's one universal magnitude axis for all numeric
tokens") was falsified with about twenty minutes of compute and one
transfer measurement. The Pythia comparison was the same shape — running
the same probe on a second model with untied weights corrected a number
I would otherwise have over-interpreted. Cheap probes are how you both
rescue and kill ideas.
